Segment 1 · 25 min · Probability as Degrees of Belief

A score is a bet you can audit.

Probability is not a mystery and not a decoration. It is a price — and prices can be checked against what actually happened.

What "0.87 spam" means

200,000 scored emails. Of the 7,552 the model scored between 0.85 and 0.89, 87.4% really were spam.

So 0.87 is a bet at 87-to-13 odds. It is not "87% of this email", and it is not "the model is 87% accurate."

The sentence that hides a condition

"It catches 99% of fraud" means: of the transactions that are fraud, 99 in 100 raise an alert. P(alert | fraud) = 0.99

Now flip it: of the transactions that raised an alert, how many are fraud? Write a number down. Don't say it.

The flip needs one thing the vendor's sentence never mentioned: how much fraud there is in the first place. That is the base rate — and it is the next 25 minutes.

Segment 3 · 20 min · Correlation ≠ Causation

Four explanations. The chart shows one.

"A moves with B" — why?

  • A → B — the one they are claiming
  • B → A — the arrow points the other way
  • C → both — a confounder drives the pair
  • Coincidence — you searched until something fit

All four produce the same chart. Three demos below; in each one we wrote the generator, so we know the true answer — the luxury you never get in a client's data.

Confounder · ice cream and drownings

312 weeks: correlation 0.94. Hold temperature still and it collapses to −0.02. The relationship was summer, and summer was never a column.

Coincidence at scale · Tyler Vigen's trick

500 pure-noise metrics × 12 months: best |r| = 0.86, and 11% clear 0.5. At 120 months the best falls to 0.28. Small n is what makes the search work.

Self-selection · the business trap

"Customers who use feature X churn 40% less" — 12.2% vs 20.3%, and true. At equal health score the gap is +0.2 points. X marks engaged customers; it does not make them.

One question per trap: "What else moves with both of these?" · "How many other things did you check?" · "Who chose to be in that group, and why?"

Segment 4 · Debrief · 12 min

Five questions — and one of them is the whole job.

  • Seasonality — what did revenue do last Nov–Dec, without the widget?
  • What else changed? — ad spend, promotions, pricing, a redesign?
  • Self-selection — who uses the widget? Engaged buyers click recommendations.
  • Was there a holdout? — real A/B numbers, or observational only?
  • The denominator — total revenue, or revenue through widget clicks?

The reveal — we wrote the generator

2024, same months, no widget existed+16.0%
Seasonality alone, before anything else+14.6%
Sep–Oct revenue, year on year, pre-launch+8.4%
The widget's real effect, via a 10% holdout+3.2%

95% range +0.9% to +5.4%. Adoption correlates 0.92 with weekly revenue — and a column that just counts weeks 1, 2, 3… correlates 0.97.

The only clean answer to "did it cause the lift?" is a counterfactual — in practice, a holdout. If none exists, phase two includes one. That is the pass bar: asking for the counterfactual, not reciting the slogan.
