Segment 1 · "When they say AI…"

Two meanings, one word.

"I want to KNOW x"

Predictive ML. The output is something you could put in a spreadsheet column: a label, a number, a group id, a ranked list.

Sounds like: "tell us which / how many / how likely / what's next."

Lives or dies on: where the labelled history is. MOD-1

"I want it to CONVERSE / write / reason"

Generative AI. The output is made of sentences — an answer, a draft, a summary, a chat.

Sounds like: "a chatbot / draft it / summarise / answer questions from our documents."

Lives or dies on: retrieval, and who owns a wrong answer. MOD-2

Three questions that settle almost every ask

1. What comes out?A number, a label, a group id, a ranked list — or sentences? Sentences → MOD-2. Make them describe one row of the output.
2. Is the answer written down for past cases?Yes — a resolved_by_team, a closed_won, a delivered_late — then it is classify / regress / propensity. No → cluster, or a labelling project nobody has priced.
3. What will they DO with it?The action names the job better than the ask does — and this is the question clients have not thought about.

Segment 1 · The Nesting Doll

ML ⊃ deep learning ⊃ generative AI.
Rings, not rivals.

"AI" — what the client says
MACHINE LEARNING — behaviour learned from data, not written rule by rule
DEEP LEARNING — many layers; for raw inputs: pixels, audio, text
GENERATIVE AI — the output is new content LLMs · chatbots · RAG · image generation  ·  MOD-2
MLP · CNN (images) · RNN · transformers  ·  LSN-1.4 trains one · LSN-1.5 names them
logistic regression · gradient boosting · k-means · the five ML jobs  ·  MOD-1
also sold as "AI": rule engines · RPA · an optimiser · a SQL dashboard with a threshold on it

Inner rings are not upgrades

Deeper is for messier inputs, not for harder problems. Solving a tabular classification with an LLM is the market's most common expensive mistake.

Two independent questions

Which job (the five) and how deep (the rings). Photos push you into the green ring even when the job is a plain classification.

"Do you do AI or ML?"

Unanswerable as asked. Answer with the rings, then ask which one their problem lives in. Thirty seconds, and the call changes tone.

Different labels (somebody had to write down 34%, and two site managers won't agree within ten points), different grading, different way to be wrong in front of a client.

Segment 2 · Jobs 1–3

Sort it · group it · guess a number.

Classify — sort it

features → one label from a known, fixed list.

Sales: which inbound buyers convert, so reps work the right list.

The money question: who already wrote the answer down? A resolver, a closed_won flag, an approval. Free labels are the difference between six weeks and a labelling programme.

Cluster — group it

features, no labels → a group id per item.

Sales: account segments marketing can treat differently.

The trap: the model returns 0, 1, 2. Those mean nothing until a human names them — so never scope "a clustering model". Scope "3–6 named groups that differ on something you'd act on."

Regress — guess a number

features → one number, with units.

Sales: what this account will spend next year. A forecast is this job with time in the features.

Say two things, always: the units, and the typical miss. A number with no error bar is a wish, and clients plan to your decimal places.

Notebook, one figure each: a fitted split line (which side is the label) · three discovered groups with a blank "name" column · a fitted line with a ±miss band in dollars. Three lines of scikit-learn apiece — props, not models. Grading is LSN-1.3.

That is a support ticket you created by omitting a range. Keep saying "the model here is a prop". Nobody should leave thinking a 240-row logistic regression is a deliverable — LSN-1.4 is where they train something real, and LSN-1.3 is where "is it good?" gets an answer. Segment 2 · Jobs 4–5 · and the arguments

Give the odds · pick the next move.
Then the boundary fights.

Propensity — give the odds

one entity → one probability. Often the same model as classify; the client acts on the number.

Why they want odds: capacity. They can work 40 deals, call 20 depots. A probability is what you sort a limited day by — and the threshold is a business decision, not a modelling one.

Recommend — pick the next move

history + context → a ranked short list with relative scores.

Read it relatively: rank 1 beats rank 2; 0.31 alone means nothing. Judged on whether the short list was useful and the human took it. Ask: how many slots are there?

The four boundaries people actually argue about — settled by what the client does with the output, never by the maths

classify vs propensityBucket or number? "Flag the late ones" → classify. "Rank tomorrow's shipments so dispatch calls the top 20" → propensity. Propensity is classify whose threshold the client owns.
cluster vs classifyHas anyone ever written the answer next to a past example? Yes → classify. No → cluster. Today's clustering is next year's classifier.
regress vs propensityUnits, or the odds of an event? Dollars, days, seats → regress. A probability of something happening → propensity.
recommend vs classifyOne winner from a handful of known buckets → classify. A ranked short list out of thousands → recommend. The tell is capacity.

Segment 3 · 20 min · The Triage Drill

Six asks, cold. One word each, in writing, first.

How this runs

  • One ask read out at a time — everyone commits a word in the notebook cell before anyone speaks
  • Then hands up per job, and the room argues
  • The instructor plays the client and pushes back
  • Answer key cell runs after the argument, never before

The pushbacks, in character

"But surely you can just tell us yes or no?" · "Can't ChatGPT do this?" · "We don't have any of that written down — is that a problem?"

Half of these legitimately move when one detail changes. What is graded is holding the call or changing it for a stated reason.

The six

  • 1Logistics client
    "We have three years of delivery records. Can AI tell us the odds a given shipment arrives late, so dispatch can intervene early?"
  • 2Retail client
    "We have 40,000 customers. Group them into meaningful segments so marketing can target each differently."
  • 3SaaS client
    "Support gets 800 tickets a day. Route each one to the right team automatically."
  • 4CFO
    "Forecast next quarter's cloud spend from usage history."
  • 5Field-services client
    "Given the job description, tell the technician which spare parts to load on the truck."
  • 6Ops director
    "We want a chatbot that answers staff questions from our 500-page policy manual."

Reserve, if the group is fast: "Can AI draft our proposals from past winning bids?"

14 min — roughly 2 min per ask plus the reserve if the room is fast. Do not put the answers on the screen; they are in the notebook's key cell and below, here, for you. KEY 1 · PROPENSITY. Three years of records means it is supervised; they asked for the ODDS and said why — "so dispatch can intervene early". Odds are what you rank a capacity-limited morning by. CLASSIFY is defensible and the lesson plan says so: if all they wanted was a late/on-time flag it collapses to classification. Push back with "can't you just tell us yes or no?" and see who holds the distinction. KEY 2 · CLUSTER. No labels exist; the model proposes groups, humans name them. If someone says classify, ask them to name the label column — then agree that once marketing names four segments and tags a few thousand customers, the NEXT project is a classifier. KEY 3 · CLASSIFY. Fixed known buckets, and the labels are free: every historical ticket carries the team that resolved it. Push back with "can't ChatGPT do this?" — the honest answer is that an LLM might be the cheapest implementation, and the JOB is still classification. Do not let the tool rename the job. KEY 4 · REGRESS. A number with units, no event to be the odds of. Accept "forecast" and "time-series" — a forecast is regression with time in the features. Add the missing half yourself: the range. Then upsell the propensity question: "the odds we blow the budget" is a different, sellable model. KEY 5 · RECOMMEND. Thousands of SKUs, one truck: a ranked short list from co-occurrence in past jobs. CLASSIFY (multi-label, one yes/no per part) is genuinely defensible and is how many real systems are built — the tell that decides it is the truck, i.e. capacity. KEY 6 · NOT ML. No target column anywhere; they want retrieval plus generation over a manual. Route to MOD-2, and reference VSP's own meeting-RAG rather than a classifier. The best wrong answer is "recommend" — retrieval really is a ranking problem (LSN-2.3) — but what the client buys is the generated answer. RESERVE: also not ML, generative — and the honest question is who signs off on a drafted proposal. Then hand over to slide 10 for the participants' own asks.
