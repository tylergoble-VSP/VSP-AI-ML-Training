Why Statistics Before Any ML

All models are wrong.
Some are useful — if you know how wrong.

George Box's line is the whole job description. Every ML answer you will ever sell is a number with uncertainty attached, and the uncertainty is the part clients get wrong.

It hides a distribution

"Average response time 240 ms" — while 79% of requests are faster than that and the worst is 11.5 seconds. The mean described nobody.

It hides a sample size

Thirty measurements and thirty thousand produce the same-looking number. One of them moves by ±64 ms run to run; the other by ±2.

It hides a collection method

Who got measured, and who never showed up in the data at all? No sample size fixes this one — it just makes the wrong answer tighter.

The sponsor's requirement, verbatim: "you're not gonna really understand what's going on unless you understand some fundamental math." This is that math — the useful 5% of it.

Segment 1 · 20 min · Distributions

A distribution answers "which values are plausible?"

A mean answers almost nothing without the shape. Two datasets you already have intuition for, same summary tool, wildly different amounts of truth carried.

Production API latency — skewed

100,000 requests: p50 = 180 ms · mean = 240 ms · p99 = 2.1 s, worst 11.5 s. Two mechanisms: a fast path and a 3% slow path of retries and cold starts.

79 requests in every 100 beat the "average". Put 240 ms on a status page and you have described almost nobody's experience.

Adult heights — symmetric

5,000 people: mean 175.5 cm, median 175.7 cm — a 0.1 cm gap, and about half of people fall below the mean.

Here the mean is the typical value. This is the case everyone imagines when they hear "average" — and it is the rarer one in production data.

When a client hands you one number, your first move is to ask for the picture — or at least for p50, p90 and p99.

Segment 2 · 25 min · Sampling

Two questions for any number:
how many, and collected how?

1 · How many? — precision

1,000 honest measurements of the same service, three sample sizes. Spread of the estimate:

  • n = 30 → ±64 ms, and runs landed anywhere from 154 to 544 ms
  • n = 3,000 → ±6 ms
  • n = 30,000 → ±2 ms

Wobble shrinks like 1/√n: to halve your uncertainty, collect four times the data. And skewed data doesn't just wobble — 57% of the n = 30 runs read low, because they missed the tail.

2 · Collected how? — bias

  • An NPS survey only angry users answer. Responding is caused by the thing being measured.
  • Latency measured only in EU business hours while the load peaks in the US evening: dashboard says 176 ms ±1.6, users got 240 ms, and p99 is understated by 83%.
  • Wald's WWII bombers. Armour where the returning planes were not hit — the ones hit elsewhere never came back.

30,000 biased samples are worse than 30 fair ones: more data made the wrong answer tighter, not truer.

Segment 3 · 20 min · Variance & Outliers

Same average. Very different promise.

Two delivery teams, both mean 5 days

Team A: σ = 0.5 days, p90 = 5.7 d. Team B: σ = 4 days, p90 = 9.8 d, worst ticket 33 d.

Promise the client 6 days: A breaks it 3 times in 100, B breaks it 32 times in 100. Whose number goes in the commitment — and what do you say about B?

One $2M deal, one broken quota

Fourteen deals averaging $80k, then one enterprise whale at $2.0M lands. Average deal size becomes $208k — 2.6×, from one deal in fifteen.

The median moves $73.5k → $75k. Set next quarter's quota on the mean and you have set it above 14 of your last 15 deals.

Three cheap fixes: report the median when it's skewed · report p50/p90/p99 instead of the centre · plot it before you summarise it.

Segment 4 · 25 min · Client Drill

"Our model detects completed milestones from site photos with 92% accuracy."

A construction-firm prospect is evaluating vendors for milestone tracking. That's the incumbent's pitch. The prospect's PM turns to you: "Sounds great — should we just go with them?"

The test set
1,000
site photos, labelled by the vendor: 920 "in progress" and 80 "milestone complete".
A model that answers "in progress" every time
92.0%
920 ÷ 1,000. It never looks at a photo. It scores the headline number exactly.
Completed milestones it finds
0 of 80
Every milestone invoice on that site goes out late — and the scorecard looks great.
92% wasn't a lie. It was the class balance of the test set wearing a lab coat — the floor, not a level of skill. And you cannot know that from the pitch. You have to ask.

Segment 4 · Debrief

Three questions before you believe any accuracy number.

1 · On what data?

Held out, or the data it trained on? From your sites, cameras, seasons and lighting — or a curated demo set? Segment 2 again: how many, and collected how.

2 · What's the class balance?

If 92% of photos are "in progress", 92% is free. Ask for accuracy on each class separately — here it's 100% and 0%.

3 · Which mistakes are the 8%?

Missed completions → invoices go out late. False completions → billing for unfinished work. They cost differently, so they are not interchangeable.

Question 3 has formal names — precision and recall, and the confusion matrix behind them. That's LSN-1.3, Grading models, where you'll do the arithmetic. Today it's enough that you never accept one accuracy number again.
