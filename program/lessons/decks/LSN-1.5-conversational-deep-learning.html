Segment 1 · A network small enough to check with a pen

Multiply, add, throw away the negatives.

input (a 2×2 patch) 0.9 0.1 0.8 0.2 ─ layer 1: three weighted sums, then the squash ─ A "left vs right" +.9 −.1 +.8 −.2 = +1.40 → 1.40 B "right vs left" −.9 +.1 −.8 +.2 = −1.40 → 0.00 C "how bright" ½(.9+.1+.8+.2) −1.2 = −0.20 → 0.00 ─ layer 2: two more weighted sums ─ vertical edge 2(1.40) − 2(0) + 0 − 0.5 = +2.30 bright blob 0 + 0 + 3(0) + 0 = +0.00 softmax → vertical edge 90.89% · blob 9.11%
Two of the three units contributed nothing — ReLU set them to exactly zero. One unit did all the work. That switching IS the "non-linearity."

Why the squash is not a detail

Without it you do not have two layers. They collapse — exactly — into one:

W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂) one 2×4 matrix, eight numbers, replacing all eighteen

Stack a hundred and you still have one. The squash is the entire reason "deep" means anything — and the notebook proves the collapse to atol 1e-12.

Say this if pushed

"Each layer throws away its negatives before passing the numbers on. Without that one step, stacking layers buys you literally nothing."

Segment 2 · 15 min · CNNs — why images want convolutions

Your model never knew
which pixels were next to each other.

Locality

Meaning in an image is local: an edge, a corner, a texture live in a small patch of neighbouring pixels. A flat row of 784 numbers has no notion of "neighbouring" — flatten the grid and the 2-D layout is thrown away at the door.

Your lab model never learned that pixel 100 sits directly above pixel 128 — and still got 92.77%. It worked despite the handicap.

Reuse

A vertical edge top-left and a vertical edge bottom-right are the same pattern. A plain network has to learn each position separately — every one of Linear(784,128)'s 100,352 weights is tied to one specific pixel.

Move the subject four pixels and, as far as that layer is concerned, it is a different input entirely.

one 3×3 filter = 9 weights + 1 biasslid over all 784 positions= 10 numbers doing 100,352 numbers' work
One idea fixes both: learn a small pattern detector, and slide it across the whole picture. That is a convolution. There is nothing else in it.

Full knowledge of where it is, four legal moves, a fixed goal. This is the cheapest routing problem that can exist — and it needed eight thousand attempts.

So the first question to a warehouse client is not about algorithms:

"Where are the robots allowed to fail eight thousand times?"

Where RL earns its keep

Games · robotics (in simulation first) · route and sequence optimisation · scheduling · control · bidding.

The common thread is never "hard problem". It is "we can afford to be wrong ten thousand times before we are right once." If every trial costs a customer or a truck, the honest answer is usually a supervised model plus an optimiser.

Precondition 1 of 2: a simulator — faithful enough that what it learns there survives the real floor. "How do you know the simulator is right?"
